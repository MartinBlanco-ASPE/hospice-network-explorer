1. What is QCOR?

Quality, Certification and Oversight Reports (QCOR) is a website of the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) with information about providers, surveys and deficiencies. For selected provider types, it provides citation-level extracts and links to survey findings.

This dashboard analyzes the hospice citation records in the study’s downloaded workbook. The statistical source is the workbook extract, rather than a new review of every linked report. The other source groups are the federal regulations and the CMS State Operations Manual.

2. Terms and source documents

Survey and survey report
A survey reviews whether a provider meets applicable requirements, using records, observations and interviews. The report documents the findings. The CMS-2567 form places deficiency statements alongside the provider’s plan of correction.
Deficiency and citation row
A deficiency is a finding that a requirement was not met. A citation row is one recorded entry in the extract. One survey can contribute many rows; a row is not a patient or a separate survey.
Deficiency tag
A tag is a code for a cited requirement. This study’s care records use L-tags. The tag identifies the requirement; the written findings describe the reported issue. A tag number alone is not a measure of severity.
Regulation text and Conditions of Participation
Title 42 CFR Part 418 — Hospice Care sets out the relevant federal rules, including health and safety requirements for Medicare participation. “CFR” means Code of Federal Regulations; “§” means section.
Survey guidance
The CMS State Operations Manual, Appendix M explains how surveyors assess compliance and connects tags, regulatory requirements and interpretive guidance. Guidance and the regulation text have different roles.
Survey group and source agency
A survey group brings together citation rows using the identifiers already defined in this study. The existing survey-group key combines the provider key, surveying organization and survey event identifier. Agencies are counted using the source CMS Certification Number (CCN); these identifiers are not independently verified agency histories.

4. How were requirement categories assigned?

A requirement category describes what the cited requirement concerns. We kept the source tag and regulatory reference, then used the existing tag-to-regulation mapping to assign one requirement category to each tag. All citations with that tag inherit its category.

The rules start with the tag’s regulation section. More specific tag, tag-range or paragraph rules take precedence when a section covers several topics. For tag-level paragraph rules, the existing mapping uses the paragraph most often cited for that tag. A broad care-planning section can therefore contain tags grouped under orders, coordination or another requirement category.

5. How were care areas classified?

A care area describes the topic of the reported issue, such as assessment, medications or discharge. The process first identifies a surveyor’s summary of the deficient practice, usually near wording such as “failed to” or “did not.” It trims introductory material and sample descriptions before applying the existing word-and-phrase rules.

A summary can match several care areas. The network retains those matches to show topics named together. For the heatmap, the primary care area is generally the first matched topic in the issue text; fixed rules handle ties and certain governance or quality-program wording. If no area matches, the record is kept under Other. Primary does not mean clinically most important.

The heatmap also keeps an IDG-uncertainty review group for 1,967 citation rows carrying the existing flag. IDG means interdisciplinary group. This review group is separate from the named care-area categories; the network retains its existing wording matches and arrow exclusions.

For example, a summary about an assessment at discharge can match both Assessment and Discharge/transfer. Its first matched topic can place it in the Assessment row of the heatmap, while the network can count both topics. The classification trees beneath the sunburst show the existing matched words.

6. How were failure types classified?

A failure type describes the kind of problem reported, such as an activity not being done, being late, or missing required information. The rules search the same issue summary for predefined wording. They can retain several matches, but select one primary failure type using a fixed order, generally checking more specific patterns before the broad “Not done” pattern.

For example, wording about completing an assessment after the required deadline can match both a timing pattern and an activity pattern. The fixed priority determines the primary failure label; it is not a severity ranking. If no rule matches, the label is Other. Some “no evidence” framing receives Not done under the existing fallback rule.

How the charts count. The heatmap counts each citation once under its primary care area and primary failure type; each cell percentage uses its care-area row total. The network counts each matched care area and pair once per citation, so care-area and pair totals overlap. Its × value is lift: the observed shared-citation count divided by the count expected from the two topics’ individual frequencies. Default network lines require at least 25 shared citations, lift of at least 1.5, and selection among either care area’s four strongest pairings.

The sunburst keeps each record’s source-cited regulation section and its assigned requirement category. Its ray length reflects distinct source agencies; its shade compares citation counts within the same regulation. In the network’s arrow view, “main” means the existing primary text label, not a clinical priority.

7. Limitations

  • Coverage. These are the findings available in the downloaded extract and retained by the study’s care-record rules. They do not include all surveys, providers or care delivered. Posting delays, source-system changes and partial years can affect comparisons over time.
  • What counts mean. Citation rows, survey groups, agencies and patients are different units. Repeated findings or similar text can appear in several rows. The source rows are preserved; no new deduplication was applied for this dashboard. Counts do not estimate patient harm or national noncompliance rates.
  • Text-based classification. The categories are reproducible word-and-phrase assignments, not case-by-case clinical judgments or a trained language model reading every report. Quoted rules, ambiguous wording, summary extraction and rule priority can change a label. Limited AI-assisted review and computational agreement do not establish independent human or clinical validation.
  • Regulatory mapping. Requirement categories reflect the existing source-linked mapping and its exceptions. A tag’s usual section or paragraph may not capture every citation’s wording. The sunburst uses record-level source sections; the requirement tree uses tag-level section assignments. In this study, one L0601 citation has a known section difference between those views.
  • Historical interpretation. Current regulation and manual links provide background. They do not establish which policy version applied to each historical survey. The categories do not by themselves determine legal compliance or policy effects.
  • Associations. A network line shows topics found in the same summary. It does not establish causation, provider relationships or statistical significance. Layout distance is a display choice, not a measure of clinical similarity. Colors and primary-label order are not severity scores.
  • Known hospice mapping issue. In the current requirement mapping, L0651, a governance tag under § 418.100(b), is assigned to service delivery (944 citation rows). The related L0650 assignment also needs review (85 rows). These assignments are disclosed and preserved; they have not been silently corrected.
  • Known hospice wording issues. Mentions of the interdisciplinary group can describe who acted rather than the care issue. Quoted regulatory wording can also trigger eligibility and symptom labels. The dashboard retains the existing review flags and uncertainty handling.
